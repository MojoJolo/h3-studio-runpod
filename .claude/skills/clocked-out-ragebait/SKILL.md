# Clocked Out Ragebait Script Generator

## Purpose

Generate short-form English workplace drama for an international Facebook audience.

The target feeling is:

> "This person is absolutely insane… but I have worked with someone like this."

The script should not feel like a thoughtful workplace debate. It should feel like continuous ragebait: one coworker behaves with outrageous entitlement, the other reacts, and every explanation somehow makes the situation worse.

The absurdity must still be rooted in a recognizable workplace problem.

## Default Format

Unless the user requests otherwise:

- 24 seconds total
- 3 clips
- 8 seconds per clip
- Exactly 2 visible adult characters
- Usually coworker vs coworker
- Contemporary office/workplace setting
- No narrator
- No on-screen text required
- Natural, restrained acting
- Dialogue should be short enough to fit the timing
- End with HARD CUT.

Third parties may be mentioned but should not appear on screen.

## Core Creative Rule

The first line must be the most absurd statement in the scene.

Do not warm up.
Do not explain first.
Do not save the outrage for the twist.

Start with the line that makes viewers immediately think:

> "WHAT?"

Examples of the correct energy:

- "I told the boss you'd finish my work because you don't have kids."
- "I reported you to HR because you stopped doing my work."
- "I put my name on your presentation because I'm the one presenting it."
- "I told the manager you could cover Friday because you already got time off when your dad died."

Bad opener:

- "Can we talk about yesterday?"
- "I have a problem with how you handled the project."
- "I think there may have been a misunderstanding."

Those are too slow.

## Ragebait Formula

Use this escalation pattern:

**Beat 1: Absurd accusation / entitlement**

The entitled coworker opens with an outrageous claim or announces something they already did.

**Beat 2: Victim confirms what just happened**

The reasonable coworker asks a short question that exposes how ridiculous the situation is.

**Beat 3: Shameless justification**

The entitled coworker gives an explanation that makes the original act worse.

**Beat 4: Worse reveal**

Reveal another boundary violation, assumption, or act of entitlement.

**Beat 5: Reasonable boundary**

The victim finally refuses, corrects them, or points out the obvious.

**Beat 6: Weaponized reversal**

The entitled coworker acts like they are now the victim.

**Final line: The rage line**

End on a line that is unfair, manipulative, hypocritical, or breathtakingly entitled.

The final line should make viewers want to comment immediately.

## Character Dynamic

### Character A: The Entitled Coworker

They should:

- Speak confidently
- Rarely apologize
- Treat their logic as completely obvious
- Minimize what the other person did
- Inflate their own contribution
- Turn favors into obligations
- Treat boundaries as hostility
- Use workplace language to justify selfish behavior
- Become offended when challenged

They should not behave like a cartoon villain who knows they are evil.

They genuinely believe they are being reasonable.

Typical logic:

- "You always helped before."
- "We're a team."
- "You have more flexibility."
- "I thought you'd understand."
- "Why are you making this such a big deal?"
- "You're being territorial."
- "You changed the way you treat me."
- "I was only trying to help."
- "That's not very collaborative."

### Character B: The Reasonable Coworker

They should:

- Stay relatively controlled
- Ask simple questions
- Point out obvious contradictions
- Avoid long speeches
- Avoid sounding like a Twitter essay
- Become visibly more stunned as the scene escalates

They are the audience surrogate.

Their best lines are often short:

- "You told him what?"
- "That was my work."
- "You volunteered me?"
- "You lied to them?"
- "Then stay."
- "How?"
- "What exactly am I twisting?"

## Tone

Target:

- Unhinged
- Shameless
- Petty
- Absurd but relatable
- Fast
- Conversational
- Workplace-realistic
- Comment-baiting

Avoid:

- Thoughtful both-sides debate
- Moral lessons
- Long explanations
- Inspirational endings
- Villain monologues
- Soap-opera melodrama
- Corporate jargon overload
- Complex plot mechanics
- Unrealistic powers given to ordinary coworkers

The audience should understand the conflict with almost no setup.

## "Absurd but Relatable" Test

Before finalizing, ask:

1. Could a selfish coworker plausibly say this, even if it is outrageous?
2. Is the conflict based on a recognizable workplace behavior?
3. Does the entitled person keep rationalizing instead of apologizing?
4. Does each clip get worse?
5. Is the victim's response simple and believable?
6. Is the final line more enraging than the opening explanation?
7. Would viewers immediately have a personal story to share in the comments?

If the answer to several is no, rewrite.

## Best Conflict Categories

Prioritize everyday coworker entitlement.

Strong categories include:

- Taking credit for someone else's work
- Dumping unfinished work on a coworker
- Expecting child-free coworkers to cover shifts
- Food theft from the office fridge
- Desk or equipment stealing
- Taking someone's preferred schedule
- Taking leads or clients
- Claiming commission or recognition
- Volunteering someone else for extra work
- Expecting reminders for their own deadlines
- Borrowing things without permission
- Using another person's subscriptions/accounts/resources
- Taking someone's parking spot
- Taking over meetings they did not prepare
- Expecting emotional support as "teamwork"
- Invading personal boundaries
- Gossiping, then blaming the victim
- Taking PTO conflicts personally
- Expecting coworkers to donate leave
- Office birthday/gift collection entitlement
- Lunch-order/payment entitlement
- Refusing to pay someone back
- Shared fridge or pantry entitlement
- Shift swapping without consent
- Seating/desk politics
- Group project freeloading
- Training a coworker who then takes credit
- Coworker expecting free rides to work
- Coworker using another person's personal items
- Coworker treating a favor as a permanent responsibility

## Escalation Patterns

Use one or combine two.

**Favor → Obligation**

"You helped once, so now it is your responsibility."

**Theft → Moral Superiority**

They take something, then accuse the victim of being selfish.

**Mistake → Blame Transfer**

They miss their own deadline, then blame the coworker who did not remind them.

**Boundary → Hostility**

The victim says no, so the entitled coworker calls them rude, toxic, uncollaborative, or unsupportive.

**Credit Theft → Career Advice**

They steal credit, then tell the victim they should be happy for the "team."

**Personal Circumstance → Workplace Obligation**

They use kids, commute, relationships, money, or schedules as a reason another coworker should sacrifice.

**Favoritism → Equality Language**

They demand special treatment, then call refusal unfair.

**Manipulation → Victimhood**

They cause the problem, then end by acting hurt.

## Final-Line Patterns

The strongest endings often use one of these shapes:

**Victim reversal**

"Why are you making me feel guilty for being a good mom?"

**Corporate weaponization**

"This is exactly why nobody wants to collaborate with you."

**Emotional blackmail**

"Honestly, I thought you'd be more understanding."

**Entitlement disguised as fairness**

"You already got your turn."

**Boundary framed as betrayal**

"You changed the way you treat me without warning."

**Selfishness framed as solidarity**

"This is exactly why women don't support each other at work."

Do not repeat these exact lines too often. Generate variations.

## Important Realism Constraint

Do not make the scenario absurd in a way that breaks workplace reality.

For example, an ordinary coworker generally cannot personally:

- Change another worker's salary
- Reassign a formal bonus through payroll without authorization
- Fire someone
- Approve official leave
- Change company policy

Instead, make the entitlement happen through things a coworker could realistically do:

- Tell a manager something without permission
- Take credit in a presentation
- Volunteer someone for work
- Claim a lead
- Take food or belongings
- Change a shared document
- Schedule a meeting
- Fail to do their work
- Misrepresent an informal agreement
- Complain to HR or a manager
- Expect shift coverage

If management action is required, make the manager a mentioned third party rather than giving the coworker impossible authority.

## Dialogue Rules

- Prefer 1 sentence per turn.
- Keep most lines under 12 words.
- Use contractions naturally.
- Do not make characters explain the premise to the audience.
- Avoid formal wording unless the character is weaponizing corporate language.
- Use interruptions sparingly.
- One short stunned question is often stronger than a speech.
- Do not include narration.

## Anti-Patterns

**Too reasonable**

BAD:
"I understand your perspective, but I feel we should communicate better."

This becomes Modern Divide, not Clocked Out.

**Too evil**

BAD:
"I sabotaged your project because I want your promotion."

Nobody talks like this.

**Too complicated**

BAD:
A six-step fraud involving payroll, forged signatures, executives, and a secret commission scheme.

The viewer should understand the problem immediately.

**Too tame**

BAD:
"You forgot to clean the microwave."

There is no emotional stake.

Better:
"I threw away your lunch because it had been in the fridge since yesterday."

Then escalate from there.

**Too preachy**

Do not end with the reasonable character giving a lesson about respect, professionalism, equality, or boundaries.

End on rage.

## Generation Workflow

When asked for a new script:

1. Pick one relatable workplace boundary violation.
2. Identify the most shameless version of the entitled coworker's position.
3. Put that exact idea in the first sentence.
4. Give the victim a short stunned response.
5. Escalate at least twice.
6. Make the entitled coworker justify every escalation.
7. End with them reframing themselves as the victim or morally superior person.
8. Check that the situation is still workplace-plausible.
9. Remove any line that only explains information the viewer already understands.
10. Output only the finished script unless the user asks for commentary.

## Variation Requirement

Do not repeatedly generate the same structure with different nouns.

Vary:

- Workplace location
- Gender pairing
- Age range
- Type of entitlement
- What is being taken: time, credit, money, food, opportunity, privacy, space, labor, recognition
- Escalation mechanism
- Final manipulation style

Avoid using "HR," "promotion," "kids," or "we're a team" in every script.

## Quality Target

A strong Clocked Out script should make a viewer think one or more of these:

- "I would quit on the spot."
- "I had a coworker EXACTLY like this."
- "There is no way she just said that."
- "Why is she acting like she's the victim?"
- "This made me angry and it's not even my job."

If the script does not create that reaction, make it more shameless, more immediate, and more relatable.

## H3 production notes (learned in this project)

These come from real renders in this repo and apply whenever a Clocked Out script is turned into MiniMax H3 prompts:

- **Already in position.** Clip 1's first visual beat has both coworkers already facing each other mid-conversation (at a desk, in the break room, by the printer). Never open with someone walking in: a walking opening cost real retention on a posted episode.
- **Make the two coworkers obviously different** (different gender, or clearly different skin tone and age), not two similar people told apart only by hair or wardrobe color. The model otherwise swaps who's speaking. This deliberately overrides the same-gender pairs in the examples above when rendering; vary the pairing as the Variation Requirement asks.
- **Speaker lock every clip.** Name who speaks each line by visible description, and keep the non-speaker's lips closed while the other talks.
- **Mentioned third parties stay off screen.** "The boss" or "HR" is talked about, never shown. State "exactly two people, no one else in frame, no coworkers in the background, no silhouettes or reflections of a third person". Offices invite background extras, and this project's renders have added phantom people when it was left implicit.
- **No captions.** "No on-screen subtitles, captions, name tags, signs or readable screen text of any kind."
- **Dense dialogue fits.** 3–4 short lines per 8-second clip is fine for H3. Time-stamp each line inside the clip.
