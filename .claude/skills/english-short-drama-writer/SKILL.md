# Short-Form AI Drama Skill for MiniMax H3

Use this skill when writing short-form AI drama videos for Facebook, YouTube Shorts, TikTok, or similar platforms.

The core goal is not literary storytelling. The goal is high-retention, emotionally immediate, easy-to-understand micro-drama that can be generated reliably with MiniMax H3 and edited into a compelling short.

The scripts should be optimized for:

- immediate hooks
- clear emotional stakes
- strong viewer reactions
- **satisfaction and shares** (the primary payload — see §0)
- retention through escalation
- a decisive status reversal as the ending
- simple generation constraints
- realistic dialogue
- international readability when English is requested
- culturally relatable Taglish when a Filipino audience is requested

**Cliffhangers are NOT the default for English drama on this account.** §0 is. The
cliffhanger patterns further down (§4C and the escalation templates) are alternatives to
reach for only when a brief explicitly asks for an unresolved ending.

## 0. THE VINDICATION FORMULA — read this first, it overrides anything below

**This is the English-drama doctrine. It is NOT the Tagalog ragebait formula, and the
two must not be mixed.** It was derived from the top-performing English posts on this
account, all of which run the same machine.

Tagalog ragebait sells **anger**: the guilty party escalates, the wronged party explodes,
nothing resolves. English drama on this account sells **satisfaction**: someone is judged
on appearance or role, stays completely calm, and turns out to outrank the person judging
them. The reversal IS the ending.

```text
JUDGED ON SIGHT -> ONE CALM QUESTION -> THE JUDGER DOUBLES DOWN
-> THE JUDGER MAKES A DEMAND -> PHYSICAL GESTURE -> FLAT STATEMENT OF REAL STATUS
-> SECOND LINE THAT OUTRANKS THE FIRST -> HARD CUT
```

**Mapped to the 3 x 8-second clip structure:**

- **Clip 1 (0:00-0:08) — HOOK / HUMILIATION.** The first spoken line must already be
  offensive enough to stop a scroll. Open ON the line — not on a polite setup like
  "Can I help you?" or "This might not be the best model for you." Too weak; see the
  opening-line bank below.
- **Clip 2 (0:08-0:16) — DOUBLE DOWN.** The protagonist challenges the assumption
  calmly; the antagonist gets a second chance to stop and makes it worse instead. This
  beat exists mainly to build anger and anticipation — it should not introduce the
  reveal's actual information yet.
- **Clip 3 (0:16-0:24) — REVERSAL / PAYOFF.** One simple fact destroys the assumption.
  No speech, no apology required. Hard cut immediately after the strongest line.

Internally: 8 seconds to make them angry, 8 to make them angrier, 8 to make it
satisfying. The series' creative identity in one line: Dhar Mann-style social conflict
stripped down into 24-second format — no narrator, no moral speech, immediate
humiliation, restrained acting, one satisfying reversal, hard cut.

### Opening lines — judged-on-sight hooks

The hook must already BE the humiliation, not a lead-in to it.

```text
GOOD:  People like you don't buy cars like this.
GOOD:  Can you get me a man who actually knows cars?
GOOD:  Economy is in the back.
GOOD:  Maybe if you were better at your job, you'd have a real career.
GOOD:  I asked for sparkling. Do I look like I drink tap water?
WEAK:  Can I help you?
WEAK:  This might not be the best model for you.
```

The viewer should be able to read the power imbalance almost without audio, purely from
who's standing where and how they're dressed — customer/waitress, salesman/plainly
dressed customer, male customer/female mechanic, wealthy passenger/casually dressed
passenger, wealthy bride/seamstress. That's what lets Shorts/Reels viewers grasp the
conflict in the first half-second.

### The four non-negotiables

**1. The protagonist NEVER gets angry.** No swearing, no raised voice, no "how dare you."
Calm is the weapon — it makes the antagonist look worse by contrast. This is the exact
inverse of the Tagalog rule, and it is the single most important difference.

**2. The protagonist NEVER defends themselves.** They ask a question or state a fact.
Defending reads as pleading and destroys the power position.

```text
GOOD:  Excuse me?  /  Is there a problem?  /  Was there something wrong with your meal?
BAD:   Actually I know a lot about cars.  /  I can afford this, you know.
```

**3. It RESOLVES.** Do not leave the aftermath open. The status reveal is the payoff and
the ending — no cliffhanger, no "to be continued," no final line that makes the
antagonist worse. That belongs to the other formula.

**4. The antagonist is never a cartoon.** They believe they are being realistic. Their
lines should be defensible to at least some viewers:

```text
I'm just being realistic.
I've been doing this long enough.
I know who's actually buying.
I've owned cars like this longer than you've worked on them.
```

### The reveal: three things that make it land

**Requested, not volunteered.** The strongest version has the antagonist *demand* the
thing that destroys them. This is the best single move in the whole format:

```text
Just get your manager.   ->   I am the manager.
```

Compare a volunteered reveal ("I rebuilt this engine last year") — same information, far
less satisfying, because the antagonist did not walk into it.

**Physical, on the symbol of the assumed status.** Act before speaking. Untie the apron,
fold it, place it on their table — *then* say the line. The apron is the thing they judged
you by, so removing it is the argument. A produced object (key fob, keys) works but is
weaker: it only proves money, not that the assumption was wrong.

**Prefer a gesture the character already owns over a produced prop.** Removing something
they are wearing (apron, hard hat, lanyard), shouldering a bag, turning to leave, setting
down their own glass — these render reliably. A small object taken out and placed
somewhere is the fragile version: a payment card laid near a dress magnetised onto the
garment twice in a row, through both a surface change and an explicit named negative.

**If a prop misrenders twice, delete the prop — do not describe it harder.** Rewrite the
beat with body language instead. Check first whether the prop is even load-bearing: the
card above was left over from a money ending, and once the payoff became "she takes the
sale away", it was carrying nothing and cost nothing to cut.

**Stacked in two lines.** Answer the demand, then escalate past it:

```text
I am the manager.        ->  And this is my restaurant.
I'm the master technician. ->  And I own the shop.
```

One line lands. Two lines land and then keep going.

**The second line must COST the antagonist something — not just prove the protagonist
has money.** Proving you can afford it is a flex and it leaves the other person
untouched. The payoff is consequence:

```text
WEAK (pure money):   That one. And the coat beside it.
STRONG (consequence): I was going to take both.
                      I'll find somewhere that would have shown me.
```

Ways to make it cost them, strongest first:

- **Take the sale away.** She walks. The refusal is stronger than the purchase, and the
  gesture inverts too — put the card down, then pick it back up and pocket it. The
  physical beat becomes the money *leaving the room*.
- **Move the transaction to someone else.** "I'd like someone else to ring it up."
  They lose the commission on the sale they refused to make.
- **Their own complaint lands on the person they insulted.** *"I am the manager."*
- **Refuse them the service they came for.** *"And we're not taking your car back."*
- **Put their judgement on record.** She is a long-standing account; this will be
  mentioned.

A money-only ending can still work (the dealership script's *"Paid cash yesterday"* did),
but treat it as the floor, not the target. Always ask: what did the antagonist actually
lose?

### Blocking carries the theme

Encode the power inversion physically. Antagonist seated, protagonist standing. Ask the
camera for the height difference explicitly. The person being condescended to should be
visually above the condescender for the whole runtime.

### Rotate the REVEAL TYPE across eight categories — money is not the default

**Superseded:** an earlier version of this section made money/ownership the default
reveal and treated occupational-authority reveals as a rare exception. That conclusion is
now replaced — rotate evenly across eight reversal types instead. Leaning on money every
time burns the format out fast ("Actually, I'm a billionaire" becomes predictable and
starts reading as hollow rather than satisfying).

1. **Status/ownership** — *"I am the manager." "And this is my restaurant."*
2. **Authority** — the protagonist turns out to hold power over the antagonist's job,
   standing, or process (their manager, their client, their examiner, the person who
   signs their paycheck).
3. **Competence** — *"I rebuilt this engine last year. I'm the master technician."* The
   protagonist is far more skilled than assumed.
4. **Financial** — *"I already bought it. Paid cash yesterday."* The classic money
   reveal — still good, now one option of eight rather than the default.
5. **Evidence** — a camera, a receipt, a message thread proves the antagonist wrong on
   the facts, not just on status.
6. **Knowledge** — the protagonist knows something private and damaging about the
   antagonist. *"Actually... yes. He was there last night. With your sister."*
7. **Seat/reservation** — the antagonist is factually in the wrong place. *"Economy is
   in the back"* lands on the antagonist's own seat, not the protagonist's — the
   aggressor's own words coming back on them is an especially satisfying variant.
8. **Relationship** — the antagonist insults someone without realizing who that person
   is connected to (the designer's daughter, the owner's wife, the reviewer's source).

**The sharpest version is still MISTAKEN FOR THE HELP.** Waiter, mechanic, valet,
cleaner, delivery driver, nanny, caddy, gardener — ordinary jobs every viewer recognises.
Being treated as the staff is a humiliation most people have actually experienced, which
is what makes the reversal land regardless of which of the eight types resolves it. Keep
the mistaken-for role ORDINARY so viewers see themselves in the protagonist; what varies
now is the reveal itself, not just the situation.

```text
mistaken for the waitress  ->  she is the manager, and it is her restaurant   (status)
mistaken for unqualified help  ->  master technician who owns the shop  (competence+status)
mistaken for the wrong passenger  ->  his ticket is 2A, hers is 32B  (seat/reservation)
plainly dressed shopper  ->  paid cash yesterday, picking the car up  (financial)
rude to a seamstress  ->  she designed the dress by hand  (competence)
threatens a waitress with "do you know who my husband is"  ->  "yes. he was here last
  night. with your sister."  (knowledge)
```

Vary the **situation**:

- luxury jewellery counter, watch boutique, tailor
- car dealership, car rental desk, showroom floor
- estate agent viewing, wedding venue, furniture showroom
- hotel check-in, airport lounge, first class cabin
- private school admissions, private clinic, members' club
- art gallery, auction room, high-end restaurant

And vary the **proof** that settles each reveal type:

- a black/metal card placed on the glass, cash or a cheque slid across the counter
- the confirmation on a phone screen turned toward them
- a key fob that unlocks the better car
- a signed contract, deed, or deposit receipt
- an apron removed and folded, a seat number read aloud, a name on a design label

Rotate the axis of judgement too: clothes, age, race, gender, accent, car, the bag they
are carrying, the neighbourhood they mention.

### Do not

- make the protagonist smug, theatrical, or triumphant — calm and matter-of-fact only
- add applause, onlookers, additional staff, or a reaction shot of bystanders
- make the antagonist comedically shocked — restrained collapse reads better
- let the antagonist apologise on screen; cut before they can
- resolve it with a threat or a firing — the fact is enough
- **stack more than one reversal** — secret wife -> secret baby -> hidden phone -> hotel
  -> second marriage -> surprise daughter starts feeling artificial. One reversal is
  enough; let the strongest line be the only twist.
- **make every ending "Actually, I'm a billionaire"** — that single phrase will kill the
  format's credibility fast if it becomes the default punchline. Rotate the eight reveal
  types above instead. The best reveals are often ordinary but humiliating: *"That's my
  seat." "I bought it yesterday." "I'm the manager." "I designed it."* — the antagonist
  simply should have asked before judging.
- let the aggressor be insane rather than believably awful — *"You disgusting poor
  person, leave this establishment immediately!"* is too theatrical; keep their lines
  defensible to at least some viewers

## 1. Default Video Structure

The default production format is:

- 3 clips
- 8 seconds each
- 24 seconds total

This format has worked especially well for:

- ragebait
- triggering relationship conflict
- cheating
- class conflict
- family conflict
- kilig
- cliffhanger scenes

Use other durations only when the scene clearly benefits from them.

Typical alternatives:

- 3 × 7–8 sec = 21–24 sec
- 4 × 6–8 sec = 24–32 sec
- 5 clips only when a slower emotional story truly needs it

Do NOT pad a short scene simply to reach 30 or 60 seconds.

Every clip MUST begin exactly like this:

```text
8 | integrated_multimodal_description: [Single continuous 8-second shot] ...
```

The duration is written as:

```text

```

Correct:

```text
8 | integrated_multimodal_description: [Single continuous 8-second shot] ...
```

Incorrect:

```text

```

Incorrect:

```text

```

```text

```

Example:

```text

overall_soundscape: ...

```

## 3. Hook First

The first clip must begin with the conflict or central curiosity almost immediately.

Avoid setup like:

- walking into a room
- greeting each other
- ordering food
- explaining who the characters are
- establishing ordinary conversation

Prefer opening lines like:

- Why did you tell her you loved her?
- You sold my house?
- How much will it take for you to disappear?
- Why are you on my husband's life insurance?
- You upgraded yourself to first class?
- Who was that woman kissing Dad at his funeral?

A strong first line should make the viewer understand:

- something is wrong
- someone is hiding something
- someone is being treated unfairly
- there is an emotionally loaded question that needs an answer

Ideally, the hook appears within the first 0.4–2.5 seconds.

### Absurd-but-realistic hooks (raw and blunt)

Some of the highest-performing hooks (validated on the Tagalog series — see the
`filipino-ragebait-short-drama-writer` skill) are almost too blunt to sound written.
That's exactly why they work: a person hurt or furious enough will actually blurt out
something this raw — it isn't composed, it's involuntary. Port the same rawness into
English rather than the more polished hook phrasing above:

- You don't even know who the father is?
- You were seeing both of us at the same time?
- So what am I to you, an option?
- Do you think I'm stupid, or just hoping I am?

These work because they're either a blunt accusation stripped of any softening, or a
self-directed rhetorical insult thrown back at the accuser ("So what am I to you, an
option?") — shocking enough to stop a scroll, but still something a real person would
actually say mid-fight. Keep it platform-safe (no slurs) while keeping the rawness.
Vary the specific line across scripts rather than reusing the same one.

## 4. Core Story Patterns

### A. Ragebait / Triggering Conflict

**Default structure: argument, not mystery box.** The strongest ragebait — and the reason Tagalog scripts in this project consistently out-trigger English ones — is built from ordinary disrespect, not a chain of hidden evidence. The pattern is:

1. One line that is already unfair or insulting on its face (no setup needed to understand it).
2. Pushback.
3. A defense that is itself the trigger — not an admission, not a twist, just a self-justification reasonable people will argue about.

Do NOT default to escalating through discovered objects (hotel receipt → secret phone → pregnant mistress → wife downstairs). That pattern reads as written/soap-opera and was the main way earlier English drafts went corny. Save object-reveal chains for when the user explicitly wants bigger soap-opera twists (see §24); the everyday-disrespect version below is the default for English ragebait.

Model examples (copy this structure, not any specific culture's phrasing):

```text
Wife: You told her you miss her?
Husband: We're just friends.
Wife: You tell all your friends you love them?
Husband: You're making this bigger than it is.
Wife: You deleted the messages.
Husband: Because I knew you'd react like this.
```

```text
Wife: You gave her money again?
Husband: She needed help.
Wife: She's your ex.
Husband: So I'm supposed to let her struggle?
Wife: You told me we couldn't afford daycare.
```

Notice neither example needs a hidden object or a reveal to land — the defense line ("You're making this bigger than it is," "So I'm supposed to let her struggle?") is what makes viewers argue in the comments (emotional cheating vs. harmless texting, generosity vs. betrayal). That argument is the payoff, not a twist.

Use:

- entitlement
- unfairness
- hypocrisy
- betrayal
- financial control
- cheating minimization
- disrespect
- classism
- family favoritism
- generational conflict
- boundary violations
- selfish relationship logic

Examples:

- husband sends money to family without telling wife
- wife or husband treats shared life like a business transaction
- adult child tries to control elderly parent's home or money
- spouse upgrades only themselves on vacation
- partner expects 50/50 money but not 50/50 housework
- one partner secretly lends joint savings
- person is caught emotionally cheating and says "nothing physical happened"
- family member assumes caregiver deserves nothing
- wealthy person humiliates working-class person

The strongest ragebait is often debatable, not cartoonishly evil.

A good ragebait character should genuinely believe they are being reasonable.

Example:

```text
That's not really my fault.
We agreed everything is fifty-fifty.
Just tell me what you want me to do.
I didn't sleep with her.
```

This creates comment wars rather than unanimous agreement.

### B. Kilig / Romance

Kilig works best when:

- only two characters are present
- the interaction is simple
- the payoff is emotional, not physical
- one character realizes the other has liked them for longer than expected
- dialogue is shy, awkward, teasing, or unexpectedly sincere

Good first-love/kilig beats:

- "So... is this a date?"
- "Can I hold your hand?"
- "I take this route because you're here."
- "You could've just asked."
- accidentally saying "I love you"
- a supposedly casual interaction revealing hidden feelings
- one person doing something inconvenient just to spend time with the other

Do not overuse:

- dramatic confessions
- kissing as the only payoff
- "I've loved you forever" monologues
- fake misunderstandings that feel forced

For teenage first-love stories:

- keep characters 18+
- make them inexperienced and shy
- keep physical affection innocent and non-explicit
- small gestures are stronger than confident seduction

### C. Cliffhanger Drama

> **NOT the default.** §0 (Vindication) is the default English structure and it
> deliberately *resolves*. Use this pattern only when a brief explicitly asks for an
> unresolved ending, and never blend it with a §0 status reveal in the same script.

A real cliffhanger does NOT simply pause the scene.

It introduces a new unanswered question that is bigger than the original one.

Good escalation:

```text
Why did you take our money?
↓
I sent it to Rebecca.
↓
Who is Rebecca?
↓
She's the reason we had the money.
↓
She wants it back tonight.
CUT.
```

Another:

```text
Who is that woman?
↓
Someone from before you.
↓
This photo was taken last month.
↓
Why is she wearing my necklace?
CUT.
```

Best cliffhanger rule:

The final line should change what the viewer thought the story was about.

Do not explain after the cliffhanger. Explicitly instruct:

```text
CUT IMMEDIATELY.
Do not explain...
Do not show...
Do not resolve...
```

## 5. Escalation Pattern

There are two valid escalation modes. **Argument escalation is the default for English ragebait** (see §4A); reveal escalation is the exception, not the starting point.

**Argument escalation (default):**

**Clip 1 — Hook**
One line of ordinary disrespect or an unfair accusation — no mystery, understandable instantly.

**Clip 2 — Escalation**
The other person's defense makes it worse. The defense itself is the trigger (self-justification, minimization, "you're overreacting"), not a new fact.

**Clip 3 — Payoff / Cliffhanger**
The accuser names the double standard or unfair logic directly, OR the defense finally cracks into one concrete admission. Either way, the climb is emotional/logical, not a chain of discovered evidence.

**Reveal escalation (use sparingly, or when the user explicitly wants bigger soap-opera twists):**

**Clip 1 — Hook**
Immediate accusation, problem, or emotionally loaded question.

**Clip 2 — Escalation**
The answer makes the situation worse.

**Clip 3 — Twist / Payoff / Cliffhanger**
Reveal information that:

- reverses power
- exposes hypocrisy
- introduces a hidden relationship
- changes who the viewer trusts
- adds a larger secret
- delivers the romantic payoff

Do NOT make every clip equally intense. The story should climb — but for English ragebait, climb through the argument getting more indefensible, not through new props or hidden facts appearing.

## 6. Two-Character Rule

Default to exactly two visible speaking characters.

MiniMax H3 can become unreliable with 3+ visible speakers.

Rules:

- maximum 2 visible speaking characters per clip
- third characters may be: mentioned, off-screen, in a phone message, arriving later, implied — never shown

Example:

```text
Your best friend messaged me last night.
```

The best friend does not need to appear. This often makes the story stronger because it creates mystery while reducing generation errors.

---

## 7. Speaker Lock

Every prompt should explicitly map speakers.

Example:

```text
Speaker lock: Only Emma, the woman, speaks S1. Only Daniel, the man, speaks S2. Never swap dialogue, voices, or lip movements.
```

If one person speaks all lines in a clip, state it:

```text
Only Emma speaks in this clip. Daniel remains silent and reacts naturally.
```

Never rely on H3 to infer who should say which line.

---

## 8. Eyeline Lock

H3 may accidentally make characters speak toward the camera.

When the scene requires characters talking to each other, include:

```text
IMPORTANT EYELINE LOCK:
Character A and Character B face each other at a natural three-quarter angle.
They speak directly to each other's faces.
Neither character looks toward or acknowledges the camera.
```

For especially stubborn scenes, add:

```text
The camera is positioned at a 30–40 degree side angle, not directly in front of either character.
Neither character ever makes eye contact with the camera lens.
No fourth-wall breaking.
```

This is especially important in:

- restaurants
- tables
- standing confrontations
- front-facing two-shots

---

## 9. Camera Rules

Prefer:

- medium two-shot
- medium close two-shot
- three-quarter side angle
- subtle handheld realism
- gentle push-in for reveal
- fixed camera if appropriate

Avoid:

- rapid angle changes
- complicated camera choreography
- cinematic orbiting
- excessive cuts inside a generated clip
- framing that encourages direct camera eye contact

Default:

```text
Single continuous medium two-shot. No cuts.
```

Use a push-in only for: key reveal, final romantic beat, cliffhanger reaction.

---

## 10. Timeline Rules

Use explicit internal timing.

Example:

```text
[0.0–0.4s] Start immediately...
[0.4–2.8s] Character asks...
[2.8–4.0s] Reaction...
[4.0–5.8s] Reply...
[5.8–7.5s] Escalation...
[7.5–8.0s] Reaction / cut.
```

Guidelines:

- dialogue must realistically fit the assigned time
- do not cram long sentences into 1 second
- leave reaction time
- reserve around 0.5–1.5 sec at the end if the scene needs a natural reaction
- for cliffhangers, cut immediately after the final reveal

---

## 11. Dialogue Style

Dialogue should be conversational, short, believable, emotionally loaded, and easy to understand without context.

Avoid:

- exposition-heavy dialogue
- monologues
- unnatural explanations
- overly theatrical speeches

Good:

```text
You told me you were divorced.
I lied.
Who is she?
You already know her.
```

Weak:

```text
As you know, we have been married for fifteen years and I trusted you with our shared financial responsibilities.
```

---

## 12. Language Rules

Use natural conversational English. Characters can be American, British, European, international, or globally neutral. Avoid region-specific slang unless requested.

> Note: for Tagalog/Taglish scripts, use the dedicated `filipino-ragebait-short-drama-writer` skill instead — this skill is English/international only.

## 13. Audience-Aware Writing

If audience data suggests viewers are 50–60+, prioritize themes like:

- marriage
- long-term betrayal
- adult children
- inheritance
- retirement money
- property
- caregiving
- family loyalty
- funeral secrets
- wills
- grandchildren
- disrespect toward parents
- modern relationship norms older viewers may dislike

A particularly strong strategy is generational ragebait.

Example themes:

- 50/50 marriage finances
- charging spouse for dinner
- husband and wife sitting in different airline classes
- splitting everything by income
- transactional marriage
- refusing to care for parents
- adult children controlling parents' property
- "privacy" arguments around cheating
- "nothing physical happened" emotional affairs

These can trigger: "Back in our day, marriage wasn't like this." That reaction is valuable for comments.

---

## 14. Poor Girl / Rich Girl Stories

The class difference should matter naturally.

Good:

- rich fiancée confronts waitress about boyfriend
- wealthy sister offers money to girlfriend
- wealthy customer humiliates server
- working-class woman knows a secret rich woman does not
- rich woman assumes poor woman stole or manipulated someone

Avoid overly unrealistic twists like: instant secret royalty, absurd inheritance reveals, improbable long-lost sibling twists — unless the tone is intentionally soap-opera.

The best poor-girl/rich-girl stories are still plausible.

---

## 15. Cheating Stories

Strong cheating hooks:

- "Why did you tell her you miss her?"
- "You told her you loved her."
- "Then why did you book her a hotel?"
- "How long have you been seeing her?"
- "Why is she wearing my necklace?"
- "You told me you were divorced."

Default escalation (argument mode, see §4A/§5): accusation → minimizing defense ("we're just friends," "you're making this bigger than it is") → the accuser calls out the defense itself (deleted messages, double standard) — no hidden object required.

Reserve the evidence-chain escalation (minimization → suspicious evidence → larger secret → cliffhanger) for when the user explicitly wants a bigger soap-opera twist.

Avoid repeating "She's just a friend" / "She's my cousin" too often. Make the excuse evolve.

---

## 16. Ragebait Character Design

A ragebait character should not behave like a cartoon villain.

More effective: defensive, entitled, self-righteous, dismissive, oblivious, believes their own logic.

Examples:

```text
That's not really my fault.
It's your half.
We agreed everything is fifty-fifty.
I didn't sleep with her.
She needed somewhere to stay.
```

The audience should want to argue about the logic.

---

## 17. BGM and Audio

Default:

```text
non_diegetic_music: None.
```

Do NOT generate separate background music for every H3 clip because music may change between generations.

H3 should generate: dialogue, room tone, traffic, footsteps, rain, kitchen sounds, restaurant sounds, clothing movement, phone vibration, glassware, ambient noise.

Add one continuous BGM track later during editing if desired. Keep `overall_soundscape` consistent across clips in the same location.

---

## 18. Visual Character Description

For independent T2V clips, repeat full character descriptions in every prompt.

Include: age, gender, skin tone, hair, facial vibe, clothing, accessories, social class when relevant.

Example:

```text
EMILY is a 33-year-old woman with fair-medium skin, shoulder-length dark brown hair, expressive brown eyes, wearing a casual knit top and comfortable trousers.
```

Do not assume the model will preserve identity based on names alone.

### Make the two characters obviously different, not just named differently

Real incidents on this account: the model rendered one character as the other mid-clip
(a wife's face sliding into her husband's; a mother rendered looking like her own
daughter). Both happened in pairs distinguished only by subtle traits — hairstyle,
hair color, age bracket. **Default to a visually unmistakable distinguishing axis**:
different gender, or different race/skin tone, or both — not two same-gender,
same-race, similar-age characters told apart only by hair length or wardrobe color.

When the scenario specifically requires two characters who share gender and race
(a mother/daughter scene, two female coworkers), compensate with:
- A real age gap with its own visual markers (grey-streaked hair, fine lines, a more
  mature build for the older character — not just a number in the bio).
- An explicit **CRITICAL CHARACTER IDENTITY LOCK** block repeated in every clip:
  name the two people must never swap faces/ages/hair/build/wardrobe/voice, and state
  which one must always look older/taller/etc.
- Still flag to the user that this pairing is harder for the model to keep straight
  than a visually distinct pair would be.

---

## 19. Character Consistency Reality

Pure text-to-video may not preserve exact faces across clips. Text descriptions improve semantic consistency but do not guarantee identity continuity.

If exact continuity becomes important, switch to image-conditioned video. Possible workflow: create one anchor image → H3 image-to-video → extract accepted last frame → use it as next first frame → only continue if the generation is good. Do not propagate a bad frame.

---

## 20. CCTV / Found Footage

CCTV is optional, not default. If requested: fixed camera, high angle, visible timestamp, fisheye distortion, compression artifacts, muted colors, no cinematic zoom, no depth of field, no camera movement.

CCTV works best for: suspicious behavior, hidden affection, cheating implication, arguments, visual reversal, "caught on camera" moments.

But do not overuse it. Normal photorealistic scenes should remain the default.

---

## 21. Sensual / Suggestive Scenes

Non-explicit adult sensuality is allowed and can be used for visual hooks.

Examples: elegant bare-back dress fastening, husband helping wife dress, close eye contact, sensual but non-explicit post-date tension, seductive conversation, woman-to-woman flirtation, post-intimacy conversation with both characters covered.

Keep: all characters clearly adult, no nudity, no explicit sexual activity, no graphic sexual dialogue.

Use sensuality as a visual hook, not as the entire story.

---

## 22. Engagement Trigger Types

Use one or more:

- **Moral outrage** — "You took my retirement money?"
- **Hypocrisy** — "Because back then, my money was ours?"
- **Class insult** — "Girls like you always want something."
- **Relationship betrayal** — "You told her you loved her."
- **Family entitlement** — "It's not technically your house anymore."
- **Generational conflict** — "We agreed everything is fifty-fifty."
- **Suspicious evidence** — "Then why did you book her a hotel?"
- **Power reversal** — Rich character insults poor character, then discovers poor character has leverage or truth.

---

## 23. Final-Line Rules

The final line matters enormously.

Strong final lines: short, specific, changes the story, easy to understand, opens a bigger question.

Examples:

```text
Your husband is.
She's alive.
She wants it back tonight.
He left his other phone at my apartment.
Your best friend messaged me last night.
Dad had another family.
```

Avoid ending with vague lines like "There's something you need to know." Better to reveal one concrete fact.

---

## 24. Realism Test

Before returning a script, ask:

- Could this plausibly happen?
- Would real people speak like this?
- Is the conflict understandable in under 3 seconds?
- Is someone behaving irrationally just to force the plot?
- Is the twist too soap-opera for the requested tone?
- Is the final reveal connected to the original conflict?
- Does this escalate through a chain of discovered objects (receipt/phone/mistress) instead of the argument itself getting more indefensible? If so and the user didn't ask for soap-opera, rewrite as argument escalation (§4A/§5).

If the user requests "realistic," keep the twist grounded. If the user explicitly wants wild soap-opera ragebait, bigger twists are acceptable.

## 26. Final Quality Checklist

Before returning any script, verify:

- The first clip has a hook in the first 0–3 seconds.
- There are no more than 2 visible speaking characters.
- Dialogue is short and natural.
- Speaker locks are explicit.
- Eyelines are explicit.
- Characters do not look into the camera unless intentionally requested.
- Physical actions are simple enough for H3.
- Each clip has explicit second-by-second timing.
- The conflict escalates between clips.
- The last clip contains a strong payoff or cliffhanger.
- The final reveal is concrete, not vague.
- The story is understandable without prior context.
- The scene can stand alone.
- The characters are distinct.
- The premise is not recycled too closely from the previous script.
- `non_diegetic_music: None.` is set unless the user requests otherwise.
- Multiple clips are separated with `=====`.
- Each clip begins with `<duration> | integrated_multimodal_description:`.
- No separate scene-header line is added before the duration.
- The duration value matches the duration stated in the shot description.
